Most agency websites try to do too much. They open with a clever line nobody understands, hide the services three clicks deep, and end with a contact form that feels like a chore. The result is a site that looks impressive in a portfolio review and does very little for the business itself.

A good agency website has one job: help the right visitor decide, quickly, that you are worth talking to. Everything else is decoration. This guide walks through the choices that actually move that decision along, in the order a visitor meets them.

## Start with the visitor, not with yourself

Open your homepage and read the first screen as if you have never heard of your agency. Does it tell you what you do, who it is for, and what happens next? If the answer is no, the design is working against you.

Visitors arrive with a problem in mind — a website that does not sell, a brand that looks dated, ads that burn money. They are not looking for your company history. They are looking for a sign that you understand their problem and have solved it before. Lead with that. A plain sentence such as "We build fast websites for growing businesses" does more work than a paragraph of adjectives.

This does not mean your site should be boring. It means the interesting part — your taste, your craft, your point of view — should show up in the work itself, not in a slogan that needs decoding.

## Say what you do in one sentence

Every agency offers a long list of services, but visitors remember one clear promise. Pick the sentence that describes your core offer and put it where nobody can miss it: at the top of the homepage, in a size that is easy to read on a phone.

Then use the rest of the page to prove it. If you say you build websites that sell, show a store, explain what changed, and let the visitor see the result. If you say you run ads that pay for themselves, explain how you measure that, in plain words. A claim without evidence is just a claim, and visitors have seen plenty of those.

One useful test: ask someone outside your team to read the homepage for ten seconds and tell you what the agency does. If they hesitate, the message needs simplifying, not a new font.

## Show the work before you describe it

People hire agencies because of what they have made, not what they say about themselves. Your best projects should appear early on the homepage, with real images and a short story for each one: what the client needed, what you built, and what changed afterwards.

You do not need dozens of case studies. Three strong ones, told honestly, beat twenty thumbnails with no context. For each project, answer three questions in a few lines: what was the problem, what did we do, and what is different now? Avoid numbers you cannot stand behind. A clear before-and-after, or a quote from a real client, is worth more than a vague percentage.

If some of your work is still in progress or cannot be shown, say so plainly. A short note explaining that recent projects are under wraps is more trustworthy than a gallery full of placeholder images.

## Make contact the easiest thing on the page

The most common failure on agency websites is a contact step that feels like a test. A form with twelve required fields, a phone number hidden in the footer, and no indication of what happens after you press send will quietly lose you enquiries.

Keep the first step small. Name, email, and a short message are enough to start a conversation. Everything else — budget, timeline, the details of the project — can come later, once you have replied. Tell visitors what happens next: "We reply within one business day" sets an expectation and builds trust before anyone has typed a word.

Offer more than one way to reach you. Some people prefer email, others would rather message on WhatsApp, and some want to call. Put the phone number and email where they are easy to find, not only in the footer. Every page should have a clear next step, not just the contact page.

## Speed and mobile are not extras

More than half of your visitors will arrive on a phone, often on a slow connection. If your site takes five seconds to load, or the text is too small to read without pinching, most of them will leave before they see your work.

Speed is a design decision, not a technical afterthought. Large uncompressed images, videos that play automatically, and animations that fire on every scroll all cost time. Compress your images, keep the homepage focused, and test the site on a real phone over a mobile connection — not just on your office wifi.

The same applies to layout. Buttons should be big enough to tap, menus should work with one thumb, and nothing important should sit behind a hover effect that does not exist on a touchscreen. A site that is pleasant to use on a phone is pleasant to use everywhere.

## A checklist before you launch — or relaunch

Use this list the next time you review your own site. It takes about twenty minutes, and it catches most of the problems we see on agency websites.

- Read the homepage headline out loud. Could a stranger repeat what you do?
- Find your services page in two clicks or fewer from the homepage.
- Check that your three best projects appear on the homepage with real images.
- Fill in your own contact form on a phone. Count the required fields.
- Confirm your phone number and email appear on every page, or at least in the header.
- Load the site on a phone over mobile data and time it.
- Click every link in the navigation and the footer. Fix the ones that go nowhere.
- Read the site for typos, especially names, prices, and dates.
- Ask one person outside your team to find how to contact you in under thirty seconds.

## The takeaway

A better agency website is not a bigger one. It is a clearer one: one sentence that says what you do, proof that you have done it well, and a contact step that takes less than a minute. Build that foundation first. The clever animations and the new colour palette can wait — they work best on a site that already does its job.
