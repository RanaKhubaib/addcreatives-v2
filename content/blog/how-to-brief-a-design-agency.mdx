Most design projects do not fail because the designer lacked talent. They fail because the brief lacked clarity. The agency guessed what the client wanted, the client assumed the agency already knew, and three rounds of revisions later everyone is frustrated — and the result still is not right.

A good brief is not a long document. It is a short, honest one that answers the questions a designer will need before they start. Writing it takes an hour. Skipping it can cost weeks. Whether you are hiring an agency for a website, a logo, or a full brand, here is what to include.

## Start with the goal, not the deliverable

The most common mistake in a brief is starting with what you want made — "we need a website" — instead of what you want it to do. A website can do many jobs: bring in enquiries, sell products, explain a service, or build trust before a sales call. Each one needs a different design.

Write down the single most important outcome in one sentence. "We want visitors to book a consultation" is a goal. "We want a modern website" is not — it is a wish, and every person in the room will picture something different. When the goal is clear, the designer can make decisions that serve it, and you can judge the work against it instead of against personal taste.

If you have numbers, share them. How many enquiries do you get now? How many do you need? What is a customer worth to you? Designers are not mind readers, but they are good at building towards a number once they know what it is.

## Describe your customers in plain words

Your designer needs to know who the work is for, because a website for busy parents looks different from a website for corporate buyers. "Everyone" is not an audience. Describe the two or three kinds of people who matter most: who they are, what they need, and what worries them before they buy.

Include the practical details too. Do your customers mostly visit on a phone or a computer? Do they prefer to call, message on WhatsApp, or fill in a form? Do they read English, Urdu, or both? A designer who knows these answers will build something your customers can actually use, instead of something that only looks good in a presentation.

If you have existing customers, tell the agency what they say about you — the exact words, if you have them. Real customer language is the best material a designer can work with, and it is far more useful than a list of adjectives like "premium" or "innovative".

## Show examples, and say what you like about them

Examples are the fastest way to close the gap between what you imagine and what the designer imagines. Collect three to five websites, logos, or brands you like, and for each one write a sentence about what you like: the colours, the layout, the way the photos look, the tone of the words.

Equally useful are examples of what you do not like, with the same short note. "Too dark", "too much text", "the menu is confusing" — these short comments save more time than a paragraph of abstract direction. Designers would much rather hear "we hate this" before the work starts than after the first draft.

Be honest about where your own materials stand. If your logo is ten years old and you love it, say so. If your photos are poor quality and you know it, say that too — the agency can plan a photo shoot or suggest stock images, but only if they know the problem in advance.

## Be clear about scope, budget, and timing

Nothing wastes more time than a project that starts before the budget is discussed. You do not need to reveal your exact ceiling in the first message, but you do need to give the agency a realistic range to work with. A designer who knows the budget can suggest the right scope; one who does not will guess, and the guess is usually wrong in one direction or the other.

List what is included and what is not. Do you need the agency to write the content, or will you provide it? Do you need photos, or do you have them? Who will update the site after launch? Each of these changes the price and the timeline, and discovering them halfway through the project causes delays and extra cost for everyone.

On timing, give the real deadline and the reason behind it. "We launch at the trade show on 15 March" is useful information; "as soon as possible" is not. A real date lets the agency plan properly, and it lets you know early if the date is not achievable.

## Agree on how decisions will be made

Many projects slow down not during design, but during approval. The designer sends a draft, three people reply with different opinions, and nobody knows who has the final say. Decide this before the project starts.

Name one person who gives feedback and approves the work. Other people can comment, but one person decides. Give feedback that is specific and tied to the goal: "this headline does not explain what we do" is useful; "I don't like it" is not. And agree on how many rounds of changes are included, so nobody is surprised when extra rounds cost extra.

A good agency will welcome this clarity. It protects their time, and it protects your budget. The projects that run smoothly are almost always the ones where the decision process was agreed on day one.

## A checklist for your brief

- One sentence stating the single most important outcome.
- A plain description of your two or three main customer types.
- Three to five examples you like, with a note on what you like about each.
- One or two examples you dislike, with a note on why.
- Your budget range, and what you expect it to include.
- Who provides content and photos, and who updates the site after launch.
- The real deadline, and the reason behind it.
- The name of the one person who approves the work.
- Your existing brand materials: logo files, colours, fonts, if you have them.

## The takeaway

A brief is not paperwork. It is the difference between a designer guessing and a designer knowing. Spend an hour writing down your goal, your customers, your examples, and your budget — and the project that follows will be faster, cheaper, and much closer to what you actually wanted.
